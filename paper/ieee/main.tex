\begin{abstract}
Drones increasingly operate where the Global Positioning System (GPS) is jammed
or spoofed. Time-Difference-of-Arrival (TDOA) multilateration from ground
beacons is a GPS-independent alternative, but only as reliable as the radio
channel carrying its signals. We reproduce a deep-Q-network (DQN) anti-jamming
model and benchmark it against training-free cryptographic frequency hopping and
spread-spectrum baselines under one simulator, a ten-class jammer taxonomy from
narrowband to wideband barrage, and a common metric suite, with fixed seeds and
released datasets. The maximum-likelihood TDOA estimator attains the
Cram\'er--Rao bound in line-of-sight from $10$\,dB upward, and RANSAC cuts
non-line-of-sight median error by up to $20\times$. The learned model beats
cryptographic hopping at small channel counts (avoidance \dqnBudgetTen\ vs
\cryptoScale\ at $M{=}10$) but fails as channels scale: at $M{=}1000$ its
avoidance is \dqnStepKilo\ even with the $15{,}000$ training updates that bring
it to \dqnStepTen\ at $M{=}10$. In a representative run, training collapses its
policy onto one channel that an adaptive jammer tracks, whereas the same network
untrained avoids \collapseUntrained\ of slots. Cryptographic hopping needs no
training, holds its avoidance as the band widens, and reaches
$\approx\!\cryptoLearnHi$ against an intelligent jammer that defeats predictable
hopping. A valid signature does not stop a delayed replay; an
authenticated-timing layer rejects every simulated spoofed and replayed packet,
provided the drone can predict each beacon's time of flight to within
nanoseconds. Cryptographic hopping costs an estimated $13$\,pJ per decision,
roughly $700{,}000$ times less than deep-learning inference. We release the code
and data.
\end{abstract}

% ================================================================= 1. INTRO
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\IEEEPARstart{U}{nmanned} aerial vehicles now perform safety-critical roles,
inspection, delivery, search-and-rescue, and defense, that demand reliable
positioning even when an adversary is actively denying it. GPS signals are weak
and unauthenticated, so low-cost jammers can deny them~\cite{ref18} and
spoofers can inject a false location~\cite{ref24,ref25,ref19}. A robust,
GPS-independent capability is therefore essential. TDOA multilateration
provides one: a drone measures the differences in arrival time of signals from
beacons at known positions and solves a hyperbolic system for its own location,
requiring neither GPS nor an on-board clock~\cite{ref6,ref8,ref30}. Its
resilience, however, is only as strong as the radio channel carrying the
positioning signals. If those signals are jammed no fix is possible, if a
beacon is spoofed the fix is wrong, and if a genuine signal is recorded and
replayed with a delay the timing that TDOA relies upon is silently corrupted.
Securing the channel is thus inseparable from securing the position.

Two communities offer competing answers. Academic work increasingly applies
deep learning to detect jamming and adaptively hop to clean
channels~\cite{ref11,ref12,ref13,ref31}. Fielded systems that must survive real
jamming instead use cryptographically-driven frequency hopping, in which a
shared secret key generates an unpredictable hop
sequence~\cite{ref1,ref2,ref17}. The learned approach is data-driven, adaptive,
and avoidance-based; the cryptographic approach is proven, training-free, and
unpredictability-based. To our knowledge, no prior work has placed the two
side by side, under an identical environment, for drone positioning, nor used
cryptographic hopping to secure a TDOA position estimate against the full
jam/spoof/replay threat. We therefore ask three questions. Under identical
conditions, when does a learned anti-jammer outperform cryptographic hopping?
Why does it stop doing so as the band widens? And can the channel that carries
the positioning signals also protect the position estimate itself?

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Let the drone position be $p\in\mathbb{R}^3$ and let beacons
$s_0,\dots,s_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ be known. With $r_i=\lVert p-s_i\rVert$, the
measured range differences relative to reference beacon $0$ are
\begin{equation}
d_i = (r_i - r_0) + n_i, \qquad i=1,\dots,N-1,
\label{eq:tdoa}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is estimation noise; the position estimate is the maximum-likelihood
solution
\begin{equation}
\hat p = \arg\min_{p}\; \big(d - h(p)\big)^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}\big(d - h(p)\big),
\label{eq:ml}
\end{equation}
with $h_i(p) = \lVert p-s_i\rVert - \lVert p-s_0\rVert$. The positioning
signals are carried on one of $M$ channels chosen each slot $t$ by a hopping
policy $c_t$; a jammer denies a set $J_t\subseteq\{0,\dots,M-1\}$. The security
objective is to maximize the jamming-avoidance rate
\begin{equation}
A = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\!\left[c_t\notin J_t\right]
\label{eq:avoid}
\end{equation}
while keeping the estimate~\eqref{eq:ml} authentic under spoofing and replay,
and while remaining tractable as $M$ grows toward the $2{,}320$-channel
configuration of fielded frequency-hopping radios~\cite{ref17}.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An open, reproducible benchmark with released labeled
spectrum-occupancy and TDOA datasets, a ten-class jammer taxonomy spanning
narrowband through wideband barrage, and a single simulator hosting every
method so that the hopping strategy is the only variable that changes.
\item To our knowledge, the first controlled head-to-head of cryptographic hopping against a
reproduced DQN anti-jamming model under one environment and metric suite.
\item An empirical model-scaling characterization under two training regimes,
equal wall clock and equal gradient steps, separating degradation caused by a
compute budget from degradation intrinsic to the learning problem, together
with a diagnosis of that intrinsic failure: at large channel counts, training
collapses the learned policy onto a single trackable channel before it has
seen enough samples per channel to learn.
\item An authenticated-timing layer that addresses the replay/meaconing gap left
open by hopping and by message signatures alike, integrated with rather than
replacing an existing protocol stack, together with the timing and positioning
accuracy it requires of the drone.
\item A per-decision cost and energy analysis, and an explicit account of the
operational limits of the cryptographic approach.
\end{enumerate}

% ================================================================= 2. RELATED
\section{Related Work}\label{sec:lit}
\subsection{TDOA positioning for GPS-denied UAVs}
Hyperbolic multilateration from arrival-time differences is a mature
localization principle: the Chan--Ho closed form~\cite{ref6} gives a
near-maximum-likelihood estimate, Foy's Taylor-series method~\cite{ref8}
refines it, and GCC-PHAT~\cite{ref7} estimates the delays; the CRLB bounds the
achievable accuracy~\cite{ref27,ref29,ref30}, and recursive filters track the
trajectory~\cite{ref26}. Recent work applies TDOA to UAVs and characterizes
altitude, bandwidth, and NLOS bias~\cite{ref9,ref10,ref15}.
\emph{Limitation.} This line assumes a benign channel and does not secure the
positioning waveform against an active jamming, spoofing, or replay adversary.

\subsection{Deep-learning anti-jamming}
Deep reinforcement learning and convolutional models learn to sense the spectrum
and hop to clean channels~\cite{ref11,ref12,ref13,ref31}, building on DQN
control~\cite{ref35} and learned RF signal processing~\cite{ref33,ref34}.
\emph{Limitation.} A learned policy needs labeled data and training, is
predictable to an intelligent adversary, and its action space grows with the
channel count, so scalability to wide-band systems is unaddressed. We reproduce
the DQN with coarse-grained spectrum prediction of Zhang et al.~\cite{ref11}
as the learned arm of the comparison, since it specifies its ten-channel
environment and jammer types completely enough to re-implement.

\subsection{Cryptographic and spread-spectrum hopping}
Cryptographic frequency hopping and spread spectrum are decades-old, proven
anti-jamming techniques~\cite{ref1,ref4,ref5,ref32}; uncoordinated and keyed
spread spectrum provide jamming resistance and key
establishment~\cite{ref2,ref16}, and follower-jamming limits are
known~\cite{ref3}. \emph{Limitation.} These are studied as communication
primitives; none is benchmarked against a learned model under one environment,
nor applied to secure a TDOA \emph{position} estimate. Cryptographic hopping is
adopted here unchanged, as a proven reference point rather than a contribution.

\subsection{GPS spoofing and meaconing}
Spoofing and meaconing (delayed replay) are well-documented GNSS
threats~\cite{ref24,ref25}, and machine learning has been used to detect
spoofing on UAVs~\cite{ref14}; distance-bounding~\cite{ref23} and message
authentication~\cite{ref21} are relevant primitives. \emph{Limitation.} A valid
content signature does not protect arrival-time integrity, so replay can
corrupt TDOA timing without forging anything, a gap not closed by prior work.

% ================================================================= 3. MODEL
\section{System and Threat Model}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Range noise and the CRLB}
The per-range estimation standard deviation follows the time-of-arrival
bound~\cite{ref27}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_r = c\,\sigma_\tau, \quad
\sigma_\tau = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\,\pi\,\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}}\;\beta},
\quad \beta = \frac{B}{\sqrt3},
\label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
with $c$ the speed of light, $B$ the bandwidth, and $\beta$ the RMS bandwidth.
The range-difference covariance is
$\Sigma=\sigma_r^2(I+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top)$, and with Jacobian
$G_{i}=u_i-u_0$ ($u_k$ the unit vector to beacon $k$) the position CRLB is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}(p) = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\!\big[(G^\top\Sigma^{-1}G)^{-1}\big]},
\quad
\eta = \frac{\mathrm{CRLB}}{\mathrm{RMSE}} .
\label{eq:crlb}
\end{equation}
The estimator solves~\eqref{eq:ml} by a Chan--Ho initializer~\cite{ref6}
followed by damped (Levenberg--Marquardt) Taylor refinement~\cite{ref8}; under
NLOS a RANSAC consensus over beacon subsets~\cite{ref28,ref38} rejects biased
beacons.

\subsection{Hopping strategies}
Both ends derive the channel from an AES-128 keystream~\cite{ref20,ref22} under
the shared key $\kappa$ and a synchronized counter,
\begin{equation}
c_t = \big(\mathrm{AES}_\kappa(t) \bmod 2^{32}\big) \bmod M ,
\label{eq:crypto}
\end{equation}
which is statistically uniform and unpredictable to any adversary lacking
$\kappa$. Direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) and uncoordinated spread
spectrum (USS)~\cite{ref2} provide the spread-spectrum baselines, and a public
pseudo-random hopper and a fixed channel model keyless, predictable strategies.
DSSS is modeled at the link level by its processing-gain margin: it delivers
full throughput while the jammed fraction of the band stays below a tolerance
of $0.34$, and degrades beyond it. The tolerance is a modeling parameter, not a
measured receiver figure.

\subsection{Jammer taxonomy and threat model}
Ten jammer classes span three tiers. Tier~1 is non-adaptive (constant, random
channel, pulsed, and wideband barrage); Tier~2 is patterned (sweep, comb,
partial-band); Tier~3 is intelligent (reactive follower, predictive learning
jammer, smart partial-band). The partial-band and smart partial-band jammers
each deny a contiguous $30\%$ of the band; the smart variant centers that block
on the transmitter's most-used channel. The barrage class denies a fresh random
$70\%$ of the band each slot. That fraction is set above the DSSS tolerance so
that the taxonomy spans both sides of the processing-gain margin: barrage is
the only class that exceeds it, and every narrowband class sits below it. The adversary may additionally
inject forged beacon packets (spoofing) or capture and re-transmit genuine
packets with an added delay (meaconing replay), the latter corrupting
arrival-time integrity without forging any content.

% ================================================================= 4. METHOD
\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Learned anti-jamming model}
The DQN observes a sliding window of the per-channel occupancy,
$s_t\in\{0,1\}^{W\times M}$, and estimates $Q(s_t,a;\theta)$ with one output per
channel; it acts greedily $c_t=\arg\max_a Q$ and is trained on the immediate
reward $r_t=\mathbf 1[c_t\notin J_t]$ (one-step / coarse-grained spectrum
prediction, $\gamma{=}0$) by minimizing $\big(Q(s_t,c_t;\theta)-r_t\big)^2$. The
output layer has $M$ units, so the parameter count and per-decision cost grow as
$O(M)$. Exploration is $\epsilon$-greedy, with $\epsilon$ decaying by a factor of
$0.9995$ per step from $1$ to a floor of $0.05$. Every $2000$ updates the greedy
policy is evaluated over one $500$-slot episode; a run converges at the first
evaluation that reaches $0.90$ avoidance.

\subsection{Key management}\label{sec:keys}
The hop sequence in~\eqref{eq:crypto} is only as available as its key. The
baseline threat model assumes pre-shared, pre-provisioned keys loaded before the
mission, which is standard practice for keyed frequency hopping and analogous to
crypto-fill in fielded radios; no over-the-air exchange is then required. Where
a key must be established without a pre-shared secret under active jamming,
jamming-resistant key establishment via uncoordinated frequency
hopping~\cite{ref16} applies, and authenticated Diffie--Hellman covers a secure
pre-flight channel. Rekeying is driven by the same counter, yielding fresh
subkeys per epoch. The security of the scheme rests on key secrecy, which we
state as an explicit assumption and revisit in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Hop synchronization}\label{sec:sync}
Correct de-hopping requires beacon and drone to agree on the current hop index.
Synchronization is ensured at three levels. First, TDOA already requires the
beacons to share a common, disciplined timebase, since they are fixed
infrastructure at known coordinates, so the hop clock reuses that timebase and
introduces no new synchronization burden. Second, the drone acquires initial
synchronization through an acquisition/sync-word (late-entry) sequence and
thereafter advances its counter locally. Third, periodic sync-refresh slots and
a tolerance window absorb clock drift and time-of-flight offset. The tolerance
is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:results} as a function of hop rate.

\subsection{Authenticated timing}\label{sec:auth}
Cryptographic hopping resists predictive jamming (its sequence is
unpredictable) and spoofing-by-injection (wrong channel and no valid tag) but
not replay: an
attacker re-transmits a genuine packet with an added delay, corrupting the TDOA
timing without forging anything. The layer in Algorithm~\ref{alg:auth} composes
three standard primitives, an HMAC~\cite{ref21}, a freshness nonce, and a
time-of-flight plausibility test in the manner of distance
bounding~\cite{ref23}, for the specific purpose of protecting a
\emph{time-of-arrival} measurement rather than message content. It is deployed
alongside whatever authentication an existing protocol stack already provides
and replaces none of it; Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies why the stack's
own signature is not sufficient on its own.

The layer is demanding in two ways. First, the time-of-flight test needs the
drone to predict each beacon's geometric delay $\delta$, which depends on its own
position, and needs its clock aligned with the beacon timebase, both to within
$\tau$. At $20$\,dB, $\tau$ is $5.8$\,ns, or $1.75$\,m of range. The drone's own
TDOA fix is too coarse to supply $\delta$ at that tolerance ($7.8$\,m RMSE at
$20$\,dB, Section~\ref{sec:results}). A deployment therefore needs an independent
prediction, for example a tracking filter with inertial aiding, or a tolerance
widened to its position uncertainty, which in turn admits replays delayed by
less than that uncertainty. Second, the freshness nonce requires the drone to
send a challenge, so the layer gives up the passive, receive-only operation
TDOA otherwise allows. The nonce defeats a replay of a previously captured
packet; a real-time rebroadcast carries the current nonce and is stopped only by
the time-of-flight test.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Authenticated-timing verification at the drone}\label{alg:auth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require packet $(\text{id},T_{tx},\text{nonce},\text{tag})$, key $\kappa$,
arrival $T_{rx}$, geometric delay $\delta$, issued nonce $\nu$, tolerance
$\tau=3\sigma_r/c$
\Ensure accept / reject
\If{$\text{tag}\neq \mathrm{HMAC}_\kappa(\text{id}\,\|\,T_{tx}\,\|\,\text{nonce})$}
      \State \Return reject \Comment{forged or altered}
\EndIf
\If{$\text{nonce}\neq\nu$} \State \Return reject \Comment{stale / replayed}
\EndIf
\If{$\lvert T_{rx}-(T_{tx}+\delta)\rvert > \tau$}
      \State \Return reject \Comment{delayed re-transmission}
\EndIf
\State \Return accept
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}
The jamming-avoidance rate $A$ is given by~\eqref{eq:avoid}; positioning error
is $\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_i \lVert \hat p_i - p_i\rVert^2}$ with
efficiency $\eta$ from~\eqref{eq:crlb}; attack success is
$\rho=\frac1K\sum_k \mathbf 1[\text{attack}_k\ \text{accepted}\wedge\text{harmful}]$,
where a replay is harmful when its added delay exceeds $\tau$, so that it
corrupts the range measurement;
normalized throughput is the per-slot delivered fraction; and per-decision
energy is $E=\text{MACs}\times e_{\mathrm{MAC}}$ for the DQN, with
$e_{\mathrm{MAC}}=3.7$\,pJ at the $45$\,nm node~\cite{ref39}, versus one AES-128
block for cryptographic hopping, taken as $13$\,pJ for a hardware AES engine
(about $0.8$\,pJ per byte).

% ================================================================= 5. SETUP
\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
The simulator is self-contained and CPU-only (Python, NumPy/SciPy, the
\texttt{cryptography} library); the DQN is a from-scratch NumPy network (window
$W{=}16$, hidden $64$, Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$, replay $2000$, batch
$32$), which makes its $O(M)$
scaling cost a measured quantity rather than an assertion. Positioning uses
$N{=}8$ ground beacons on a $1$\,km hexagon with mast-height diversity and a
drone at $\sim\!300$\,m. Spectrum episodes are $500$ slots; anti-jam results
average $30$ seeds, the equal-wall-clock scaling arm $10$ seeds, the
equal-gradient-step arm $3$ seeds, the significance tests an independent
replicate of $8$ seeds, and the policy-collapse probe one representative seed;
every run is reproducible from a fixed seed.
Channel scales are $\{10,100,1000,2320\}$, the last being the SINCGARS
30--88\,MHz/25\,kHz configuration~\cite{ref17}. The labeled spectrum-occupancy
dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}) is released with the code.

% ================================================================= 6. RESULTS
\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Positioning accuracy against the CRLB}
The maximum-likelihood estimator attains the CRLB in LOS from $10$\,dB upward,
with efficiency $\eta$ between $0.97$ and $1.06$ (RMSE $7.8$\,m against a CRLB
of $8.2$\,m at $20$\,dB). Values slightly above $1$ are within the Monte-Carlo
error of $500$ trials, about $3\%$ relative. Below $10$\,dB the efficiency
falls to $0.91$, the usual threshold behavior of the maximum-likelihood
estimator at low SNR.

These results use the analytic range-noise model~\eqref{eq:sigma}. A
signal-level GCC-PHAT check does not reproduce that model across SNR: its
empirical error stays between $0.43$ and $0.85$\,m from $0$ to $30$\,dB,
whereas the analytic $\sigma_r$ falls from $5.84$ to $0.18$\,m, and the two
agree only near $20$\,dB (ratio $0.79$). The positioning results therefore
describe the analytic noise model, not a validated signal-level receiver.

NLOS error is heavy-tailed, so we
report the median: RANSAC mitigation reduces it from $133$\,m to $33$\,m at
$20$\,dB and from $148$\,m to $7.4$\,m at $30$\,dB (Table~\ref{tab:tdoa},
Fig.~\ref{fig:tdoa}).

\subsection{Anti-jamming across the taxonomy}
Against the ten-class taxonomy, cryptographic hopping avoids collisions with a
rate that rises with $M$ (mean avoidance over the ten classes
\cryptoMeanLo$\,\to\,$\cryptoMeanHi)
and, crucially, resists the intelligent adversary: against the learning jammer
its avoidance is \cryptoLearnLo$\,\to\,$\cryptoLearnHi\ while predictable
public-random and fixed hopping collapse to $0.00$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:heat}). This is
the operational meaning of cryptographic unpredictability, and it is what a
keyless pseudo-random hopper cannot provide.

The taxonomy also bounds the spread-spectrum baseline. DSSS is unaffected by
every narrowband class, because none of them exceeds its processing-gain
margin, but wideband barrage denies $70\%$ of the band at once, drops DSSS
throughput to \dsssBarrageTput, and takes its avoidance to $0.00$. No hopping
strategy fares better in that column: with $70\%$ of the band denied, every
hopper, cryptographic or otherwise, is reduced to \barrageAvoid. Barrage is
therefore the one adversary in the study that defeats both families, which is
the expected consequence of spreading jamming power across the entire band
rather than concentrating it.

\subsection{Learned versus cryptographic hopping at scale}
The DQN, when it can be trained, beats cryptographic hopping on structured
adversaries, since it learns to occupy clean channels: \dqnBudgetTen\ against
\cryptoScale\ at $M{=}10$. The advantage does not survive scaling. Under a
fixed $20$\,s compute budget, avoidance falls to \dqnBudgetKilo\ at $M{=}1000$,
while training throughput collapses from \stepsLo\ to \stepsHi\ steps/s and the
parameter count grows from $\paramsLo$ to $\paramsHi$ (Table~\ref{tab:scale},
Figs.~\ref{fig:cross} and~\ref{fig:scost}). Cryptographic hopping stays at
\cryptoScale\ at every $M$, because this jammer always covers $30\%$ of the
band, and it needs no training.

A fixed wall-clock budget starves the wide-band models by construction, so we
repeat the experiment with the budget removed and the number of gradient
updates held at $15{,}000$ for every $M$. If the degradation were only a budget
effect, this arm would recover it. It does not: equal training gives
\dqnStepKilo\ at $M{=}1000$ and \dqnStepMax\ at $M{=}2320$ on every seed, and no
model at either size reaches $0.90$ avoidance within the limit, whereas at
$M{=}10$ and $M{=}100$ that level is reached after a mean of \convTen\ and
\convHundred\ updates over three seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:conv}); evaluations run
every $2{,}000$ updates, so each seed's value is a multiple of $2{,}000$. The
equal-wall-clock value at $M{=}2320$ (\dqnBudgetMax$\,\pm\,$\dqnBudgetMaxSd) is
not a recovery either. At $5$\,steps/s that arm completes only about
\budgetStepsMax\ updates, and Table~\ref{tab:collapse} shows that at $M{=}1000$
as few as $220$ updates already concentrate the policy on one channel. Its wide
spread across seeds is consistent with some runs having collapsed and others not
yet.

\subsubsection*{Why more training does not help}
Table~\ref{tab:collapse} probes the greedy policy the DQN would deploy at three
points in training, for one representative seed; the zero variance of the
equal-steps arm across seeds at $M{=}1000$ is consistent with it. At $M{=}100$ training eventually diversifies the policy:
after $3000$ updates it uses more distinct channels than at initialization, its
most-used channel takes a smaller share, and avoidance reaches
\collapseHundredLate. At $M{=}1000$ the reverse
happens. The untrained network avoids \collapseUntrained\ of slots, close to
cryptographic hopping, because the argmax of a randomly initialized network
shifts with its input and so behaves as a pseudo-random hopper. After $220$
updates, about what the $20$\,s budget allows at $M{=}1000$, \collapseEarlyShare\% of its choices go to a single channel, and after
$3000$ updates \collapseLateShare\%. The smart partial-band jammer centers its
block on the transmitter's busiest channel, so it covers that channel in every
slot.

The cause is sample complexity. Only the value of the chosen action is updated,
so each of the $M$ output units needs its own samples. The exploration
schedule spends about \exploreHundred\ random trials per channel in the first
$3000$ steps at $M{=}100$, but only \exploreKilo\ at $M{=}1000$. The few
channels that happened to be rewarded early then dominate the argmax whatever
the input. For the learned hopper, unpredictability is lost before competence is
gained; for cryptographic hopping, unpredictability is a property of the key and
cannot be trained away.

\subsection{Securing the position, and its cost}
Table~\ref{tab:sec} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sec} report attack-success rates over
$2000$ simulated attempts per layer. They check that each layer rejects the
attack it is designed for under the requirements of
Section~\ref{sec:auth}, rather than measuring detection under noise: the
simulation gives the drone the true geometric delay and a perfectly aligned
clock. Cryptographic hopping alone reduces injection spoofing to
$\approx\!1/M$, because the spoofer must first guess the active channel, but
leaves replay at $1.00$. Adding a message signature stops spoofing, since a
forged tag never verifies, yet a valid signature still loses to replay
($1.00$), because it does not protect arrival-time integrity. The full layer
rejects both. In our replay model the attacker rebroadcasts a previously
captured packet delayed by $20\sigma_r$ ($11.7$\,m of range at $20$\,dB), so the
stale nonce alone rejects it; a real-time rebroadcast would carry the current
nonce and would be stopped only by the time-of-flight test.

\emph{Cost.} Per decision, cryptographic hopping costs a single AES block, an
estimated $13$\,pJ on hardware AES, against $0.04$--$9.3$\,$\mu$J for DL
inference, a factor of up to $\costRatio$, and it requires no training
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}). The comparison is at the order-of-magnitude level: both
figures are per-operation energy estimates rather than measurements on one
platform.

\emph{Synchronization budget.} We model de-hopping as failing once the clock
offset between beacon and drone passes half the hop dwell, with Gaussian timing
jitter of standard deviation $5\%$ of the dwell. Holding de-hop success at
$99\%$ requires the offset to stay within about $0.37$ of the dwell: $3.66$\,ms,
$366$\,$\mu$s, and $36.6$\,$\mu$s at $100$, $1000$, and $10000$ hops/s
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sync}). The budget shrinks in proportion to the hop rate. For a
$1$\,ppm reference oscillator, which drifts $1$\,$\mu$s per second, these
budgets are used up in roughly an hour, six minutes, and $37$\,s respectively,
which sets how often the drone must resynchronize. Each resynchronization, an
$8$-slot late-entry acquisition in our model, costs $80$, $8$, and $0.8$\,ms.

\subsection{Statistical validation}
All comparisons use independently seeded runs. Differences are assessed with the
paired $t$-test and the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{ref36},
with $95\%$ bootstrap confidence intervals and Cohen's $d$ effect
size~\cite{ref37}, at $\alpha{=}0.05$ with Bonferroni correction over the
reported family. Table~\ref{tab:stat} confirms the crossover rather than
asserting a single winner: the learned model significantly exceeds
cryptographic hopping at $M{=}10$ and $M{=}100$, and cryptographic hopping
significantly exceeds it at $M{=}1000$. Against the intelligent learning
jammer, cryptographic hopping exceeds the predictable public-random hopper by
almost the full scale. Its differences from uncoordinated spread spectrum and
DSSS are statistically detectable but below $0.01$ in size; against that
adversary all three unpredictable or spread schemes are effectively immune, and
the separation that matters is between unpredictable and predictable strategies.
The DQN rows come from the independent $8$-seed replicate, trained for $8$\,s at
$M{=}10$ and $20$\,s at $M{=}100$ and $1000$, so their differences do not
recompute exactly from Table~\ref{tab:scale}: at $M{=}1000$, for example, the
replicate's DQN mean is $0.12$ rather than $0.07$.

% ================================================================= 7. DISCUSSION
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Boundary conditions}
The findings are boundary-conditioned. A learned policy is the better
anti-jammer \emph{when} the channel count is small and training is affordable
and \emph{when} the adversary is not itself adaptive to the policy; it exploits
structure that a blind keyed hopper ignores. The collapse analysis explains
where that boundary comes from. A value-based learner has to try every channel
enough times before it can distinguish them, and until it has, training does
not make it neutral: it makes it predictable, which is the worst outcome
against an adversary that tracks behavior. Cryptographic hopping wins
\emph{when} the band is wide, \emph{when} training is impractical, and
\emph{when} the adversary is intelligent, because unpredictability is a
property of key secrecy, not of data. A wide band helps it against a jammer
that covers a fixed number of channels, whose hit probability falls as $1/M$;
against a jammer that covers a fixed fraction of the band, its collision rate
stays at that fraction, which is the constant \cryptoScale\ in
Table~\ref{tab:scale}. Only the cryptographic path additionally protects the
\emph{position}: with authenticated timing, and given the timing requirements
of Section~\ref{sec:auth}, it rejects jamming, spoofing, and replay together,
which a communication-only learned hopper does not attempt.

\subsection{Limits of the cryptographic approach}
The cost analysis settles one common objection, that cryptography is as
expensive as deep learning: generating a hop index is a single AES block, orders
of magnitude below a neural forward pass and free of any training. The real
costs are operational rather than computational, and they are not free.
\emph{Key distribution} is a logistical burden: keys must be provisioned before
the mission and re-provisioned when compromised, and the pre-shared assumption
of Section~\ref{sec:keys} is exactly what an adversary with physical access
attacks. \emph{Key secrecy is a single point of failure}: a disclosed key
converts the unpredictable hopper into the predictable one, which the learning
jammer drives to $0.00$ avoidance. \emph{Authenticated timing is demanding}: it
needs nanosecond-level clock alignment and a range prediction finer than the
TDOA fix itself (Section~\ref{sec:auth}), and its nonce round makes the drone
transmit. \emph{Synchronization must be maintained}, and
a drone that loses it pays the resynchronization latency of
Section~\ref{sec:results} before it can receive at all. Finally, the
cryptographic hopper is \emph{blind}: it cannot exploit exploitable structure in
the jammer, which is precisely why the learned model wins at small $M$. Neither
approach answers wideband barrage, which defeats both.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\emph{Construct validity.} The jammer--hopper interaction is simulated at the
channel-occupancy level, and range noise follows an analytic model that a
signal-level GCC-PHAT check reproduces only near $20$\,dB; full RF propagation
and hardware imperfections are abstracted. The security evaluation gives the
drone the true geometric delay and a perfectly aligned clock, so it shows that
the protocol logic is sound, not that a deployed drone meets those
requirements. Two further consequences are worth stating plainly. The DSSS baseline is modeled by a
processing-gain tolerance and therefore represents an upper bound on what a real
DSSS receiver achieves. Conversely, the barrage jammer pays no penalty in this
model for spreading its power across the whole band, whereas a real barrage
adversary trades per-channel power density for coverage; the barrage column is
therefore pessimistic for the defenders and should be read as a bound, not a
prediction.
\emph{Internal validity.} The scaling result is reported under both a fixed
wall-clock budget and a fixed number of gradient updates precisely because the
first alone would confound the budget with the learning problem; the $O(M)$
growth in parameters and per-step cost is architectural and holds under either.
The collapse diagnosis is drawn from one representative seed, and agrees with
the zero variance of the equal-steps arm across all seeds at $M{=}1000$ and
$M{=}2320$. The significance tests use an independent replicate whose DQN means
differ slightly from those of the scaling study. It characterizes the reproduced learner, a value-based agent that
updates only the chosen action under $\epsilon$-greedy exploration. Learners
that share information across actions, factorize the action space, or
regularize their policy toward high entropy may resist collapse, and are the
natural next comparison.
\emph{External validity.} Results are simulation-based with a single beacon
geometry and a ten-class taxonomy; field behavior may differ, and
hardware-in-the-loop validation remains future work.
\emph{Conclusion validity.} Multiple seeds, paired significance tests,
confidence intervals, and Bonferroni correction guard against chance findings;
NLOS metrics use medians because the error is heavy-tailed.

% ================================================================= 8. CONCLUSION
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We benchmarked a reproduced deep-Q anti-jamming model against training-free
cryptographic frequency hopping for GPS-denied drone TDOA positioning, under one
environment, dataset, and metric suite. The learned model matches or beats
cryptographic hopping at small channel counts but fails as channels scale, and
more training does not rescue it: at large channel counts training collapses
its policy onto a single channel that an adaptive jammer tracks, so the learner
loses its unpredictability before it gains competence. Cryptographic hopping
stays effective, training-free, and unpredictable by construction, and,
with an authenticated-timing layer, can also reject spoofed and replayed
positioning signals, provided the drone can predict each beacon's time of flight
to within nanoseconds, a requirement that sets the agenda for deployment. The
maximum-likelihood TDOA estimator reaches the CRLB from $10$\,dB upward, and
NLOS mitigation recovers most of the loss.
Extending the taxonomy to wideband barrage bounds both families: it is the one
adversary that defeats spread spectrum and hopping alike. We release the
datasets, code, and benchmark. Future work extends the study to signal-level
propagation, hardware-in-the-loop trials, a time-of-flight check driven by
the drone's own tracking filter, adaptive-adversary co-training, and
learners designed to resist policy collapse, such as entropy-regularized or
action-factorized agents.
